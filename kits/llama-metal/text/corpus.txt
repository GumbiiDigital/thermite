The Keeper of the Lower Light

The lighthouse at the end of the long breakwater was never tall. It stood no higher than a three-storey house, and its lamp was small enough that a child could have put both arms around the glass. Ships did not steer by it from far out at sea. It was a harbour light, a light for the last mile, and the people of the town called it the lower light to tell it apart from the great tower on the headland. The great tower showed sailors where the coast began. The lower light showed them where the safe water ended and the rocks began.

For forty-one years the lower light was kept by a woman named Ada Fenwick. She took the post when her husband died, because the harbour board could not find anyone else who would live on the breakwater through the winter, and she kept it long after the board stopped asking whether she wanted to retire. She lived in two rooms at the foot of the tower. One room held a stove, a table, a bed and a shelf of books. The other held oil, wicks, spare glass, brass polish, a barometer, and the log.

The log was the centre of her life. Every evening she wrote the time she lit the lamp, the direction and strength of the wind, the state of the sea, and the ships that came in or went out. Every morning she wrote the time she put the lamp out and what the night had been like. She did not write about herself. In forty-one years the log never says that she was tired, or lonely, or ill, although she must have been all three. It says that the wind backed to the south-west at two in the morning, that a fishing boat came in under sail with her engine dead, that the fog lifted at dawn. It is a record of weather and work, and it is one of the most honest documents the town has.

People who read the log now are often surprised by how much happened on a quiet harbour. In a single winter Ada recorded eleven boats that came in damaged, three that needed a tow, and one that struck the outer rocks and sank, with all of her crew saved by the lifeboat. She recorded the night a storm took the roof off the fish market and the morning the harbour froze from one wall to the other, which had not happened in living memory and has not happened since. She recorded the date the electric cable reached the breakwater and the lamp was changed from oil to a bulb, and she recorded, two lines later, that she kept the oil lamp cleaned and filled in case the cable failed. It failed four times in the next ten years. Each time she lit the oil lamp within minutes.

That habit of keeping the old way ready while using the new way is perhaps the main lesson of her life. She did not distrust the electric light. She wrote that it was brighter, steadier and much less work. But she knew that a light which fails for an hour on the wrong night is worse than no light at all, because sailors trust it and steer toward a place that is suddenly dark. So she kept two systems, used the better one, and tested the worse one every week.

The Bread Oven on Mill Street

There was a bakery on Mill Street that baked in a wood-fired oven until the year the baker's grandson took over the business. The oven was built of brick and clay, with a domed roof and a floor of flat stones, and it was so large that the baker could walk into it, bent over, when it was cold. It took three hours to heat in the morning. The fire was built on the floor of the oven itself, and when the bricks were hot enough the baker raked the coals out, swept the floor with a wet cloth tied to a pole, and put the bread in where the fire had been.

There was no thermometer. The baker judged the heat by throwing a handful of flour onto the floor and counting how long it took to brown. If it burned black at once, the oven was too hot, and he waited. If it stayed white, the oven was too cool, and the day's first batch would be pale and heavy. The right heat turned the flour the colour of a hazelnut in about the time it took to say the names of the days of the week. His father had taught him that rule, and his grandfather had taught his father, and none of them knew who had first thought of it.

The bread that came out of that oven was baked by a falling heat. The bricks were hottest when the first loaves went in, and they cooled through the morning. So the baker arranged his day around the cooling. The large country loaves, which needed a fierce start to rise and form a thick crust, went in first. The tin loaves followed. Then came rolls, then sweet buns, which would have burned in the early heat, and last of all the trays of biscuits and the dishes that neighbours brought to cook in the last warmth of the oven, at a small price, because they had no ovens of their own.

When the grandson took over, he replaced the brick oven with an electric deck oven that could hold any temperature he set, all day, without a fire. His customers said the bread was never quite as good. He tested the claim fairly. For a month he baked the same dough in both ovens, before the old one was taken down, and asked people to taste the loaves without knowing which was which. Most of them could not tell the difference in the tin loaves or the rolls. Almost all of them could tell the difference in the country loaves, and they preferred the ones from the brick oven. The fierce first heat made a crust that the steady electric oven could not match.

He kept the electric oven, because it saved him three hours every morning and let him sleep. But he changed the way he baked the country loaves. He learned to start them hotter than the recipe said and to turn the heat down after ten minutes, copying the falling heat of the old oven on purpose. The crust came back. He said later that the old oven had not been magic. It had simply done something useful by accident, and once he understood what it was, he could do it on purpose.

How a River Changes Its Mind

A river looks permanent on a map, but over the span of a few lifetimes most rivers move. The river that runs past the town once flowed on the far side of the valley. Old field boundaries, which follow its former course, still curve in a wide arc that makes no sense until you know the water used to be there. Farmers ploughing those fields sometimes turn up smooth stones and lines of gravel, the remains of the old bed.

Rivers move because they are always carrying material from one place to another. On the outside of a bend the water runs fast and cuts into the bank. On the inside of the bend it runs slowly and drops the sand and gravel it was carrying. Year by year the outside bank retreats and the inside bank grows, so the bend creeps sideways across the valley floor. Bends also grow sharper, until two of them come so close together that a flood cuts straight across the narrow neck of land between them. Then the river takes the short way, and the old loop is left behind as a curved lake, which slowly fills with mud and reeds and becomes a marsh, and then a damp meadow.

People have spent a great deal of effort trying to stop rivers from moving. They build stone walls along the outside of bends, straighten the channel, and raise banks to keep floods off the fields. These works often succeed for a while. But a river that cannot spread out during a flood must carry all of its water in the channel, so the water runs higher and faster, and the damage, when the banks finally fail, is worse. A river that cannot drop its sand on the inside of bends carries it downstream instead, where it fills a harbour or blocks a bridge.

In the last thirty years the town has tried a different approach. Upstream, where the river crosses open farmland, the old banks have been lowered in a few places, so that floods can spread over fields that were bought from the farmers for the purpose. Those fields are wet in winter and good grazing in summer. When a large flood comes, the water spreads out over them, slows down, and drops much of its load. The river in the town runs lower than it would have. The harbour needs less dredging. And the fields, which flood a few times a year, have become home to birds that had not nested in the valley for a century.

The engineers who designed the scheme were careful to say that it does not stop the river from moving. It gives the river room to move where movement does the least harm. Their reports describe the river as a system that will always find a way to carry its water and its sediment to the sea, and they describe their job as choosing where that work is done, rather than pretending it can be prevented.

The Clock in the Station Hall

The railway station has a large clock above the entrance to the platforms. It has hung there for more than a century, and for most of that time it was wound by hand once a week by a member of the station staff who climbed a ladder behind the wall. The clock was not especially accurate on its own. Left alone, it gained about half a minute a week in summer and lost about the same in winter, because the long steel rod of its pendulum grew and shrank with the temperature of the hall.

For a railway, half a minute matters. Trains in the early years ran to the local time of each town, which was set by the sun, so that the clock in one town might be several minutes ahead of the clock in the next town to the west. Timetables were full of confusing notes, and passengers missed connections. When the railways agreed to keep one standard time across the whole country, every station clock had to agree with every other, and the problem of the pendulum rod became a real nuisance.

The station's answer was a telegraph signal. Every morning at a fixed hour, a signal sent along the telegraph wires from the central observatory reached the station, and the stationmaster checked the clock against it and moved the hands if they were wrong. Later, a small electric device was fitted that corrected the clock automatically when the signal arrived, pulling the minute hand to the exact mark. The clock still kept time by its pendulum through the day, but every morning its error was wiped away.

This arrangement is a small example of a common idea. A device that is cheap and good enough in the short term is joined to a source that is expensive but very accurate, and the accurate source is used only to correct the cheap device now and then. The pendulum did the everyday work of keeping time. The observatory did the rare work of keeping the pendulum honest. Neither could have done the whole job alone at a sensible cost.

Today the station clock is driven by a quartz movement that takes its time from a radio signal, and nobody climbs the ladder any more. But the clock face, the hands and the case are the original ones. Travellers who look up at it to check whether they will make their train are reading the same face their great-grandparents read, and they are, in a way, still trusting the same idea: a local clock, often corrected by a better one far away.

Notes on Learning a Second Language as an Adult

Many adults who start to learn a new language expect to be bad at it, and they are often told that children learn languages easily while adults struggle. The truth is more mixed. Young children do usually end up with a better accent, and they pick up the rhythm of a language without effort. But in the first months adults often learn faster than children. They can read, they can use a dictionary, they can ask why a sentence is built the way it is, and they can practise on purpose.

The main difficulty for adults is not memory. It is that adults are used to being competent. Speaking a new language badly feels like being a child again, and many people stop because they cannot bear to sound foolish. Teachers who work with adults spend a great deal of their time on this problem. They create situations where mistakes are expected and cost nothing: games, role plays, short conversations with a partner who is also learning. The aim is to make the learner speak a lot, badly at first, so that speaking stops being frightening.

A second difficulty is that adults try to translate. They build a sentence in their own language and then replace each word with a word from the new one. This works for simple sentences and fails for most others, because languages do not divide the world in the same way. One language has a single word for a thing that another describes with three words, or with a different verb, or with no word at all. Learners who read and listen a great deal slowly stop translating. They begin to hear whole phrases as units and to reach for them directly.

Research on how people learn vocabulary points to a few simple habits that help. Words are remembered better when they are met several times, spread out over days and weeks, than when they are studied many times in one evening. They are remembered better in a sentence than alone, and better still in a sentence the learner has made up. Testing yourself, by trying to recall a word before you look at the answer, works better than reading a list over and over, even though reading the list feels easier and more pleasant.

None of these habits is a secret, and none of them is fast. A learner who studies for half an hour a day will usually be able to hold a simple conversation after a few months and read a newspaper after a year or two. That is slower than the advertisements promise. It is also, for most people, entirely possible, which is the point worth remembering on the days when the new language seems like a wall.

The Repair Shop

At the back of the hardware shop there was a repair counter, and behind the counter worked a man who could fix almost anything with a motor, a spring or a hinge. People brought him lawn mowers, toasters, radios, sewing machines, clocks, umbrellas and once, famously, a stuffed owl whose glass eye had fallen out. He charged very little. He said that most things that broke had only one part wrong, and that finding the wrong part was the whole job; replacing it was easy.

His method was always the same. First he asked the owner exactly what had happened: what the machine did, what it failed to do, and when the trouble started. He said that half of all faults could be found from the story alone. A toaster that worked on one side but not the other had a broken element on the dead side. A radio that crackled when it was moved had a loose connection, not a failed part. A mower that started and then stopped after a minute was starving for fuel, and the fault was somewhere between the tank and the engine.

Then he tested one thing at a time. He never changed two parts at once, because if the machine then worked he would not know which change had fixed it, and if it still failed he would not know whether he had made things worse. He kept a notebook of every repair, with the fault, the cause, and the fix, and over the years the notebook became a kind of map of how ordinary machines fail. He found that the same few faults turned up again and again: worn brushes in motors, dry or cracked rubber belts, corroded battery contacts, blocked filters, and screws that had shaken loose.

He was also honest about when a thing was not worth repairing. If the part he needed cost more than a new machine, or if the machine was so worn that another part would fail next month, he said so and charged nothing for looking. Customers trusted him because of this. They knew that when he said a repair was worth doing, it was.

When he retired, the shop kept his notebook behind the counter. The young woman who took over the repairs said she still opened it at least once a week. The machines had changed, and many of them now had circuit boards where the old ones had switches and springs. But the method had not changed at all: listen to the story, form a guess, test one thing at a time, write down what you learn, and be honest about the cost.

Winter on the Allotments

The allotment gardens on the hill above the town are busiest in spring and summer, when every plot is full of beans, potatoes, onions and flowers, and the paths are crowded with people carrying watering cans. In winter they look abandoned. The soil is bare or covered with old carpet and black sheets, the sheds are locked, and the only movement is a robin hopping along a fence. But the gardeners who keep the best plots say that winter is when the next year is decided.

In late autumn the experienced gardeners dig compost and manure into the beds where hungry crops such as cabbages and potatoes will grow. The frost does useful work on that rough ground. Water in the soil freezes and expands, breaking the heavy clods into a fine crumb that is easy to rake in spring. Gardeners who dig in spring, after a winter of leaving the ground hard and compacted, spend their first weeks fighting it.

Winter is also the time for planning the order of the beds. Most allotment holders move their crops around the plot from year to year, so that the same family of plants does not grow in the same place twice in a row. Potatoes one year, then beans and peas, then cabbages and their relatives, then roots such as carrots and parsnips, and then potatoes again. The rotation keeps pests and diseases from building up in one spot, and it makes good use of the fact that beans and peas leave the soil richer than they found it.

On the coldest days some of the older gardeners still come up the hill, unlock their sheds, and sit inside with a flask of tea, sorting seed packets and mending tools. They oil the blades of their spades and shears, replace broken handles, and sharpen hoes with a file. A sharp hoe, they say, does twice the work of a blunt one and takes half the effort, and there is no better time to sharpen it than a day when nothing else can be done.

When the first warm week of spring arrives, the plots that were looked after in winter are ready at once. The soil is crumbly, the tools are sharp, the seeds are sorted and the plan is written down. Their owners sow on the first good day and are weeks ahead by midsummer. Their neighbours, who did nothing over winter, start by digging and mending, and by the time they are ready to sow, the best part of the spring has gone.

The Sound of the Town at Night

A town is never silent at night, even when everyone is asleep. Someone who lies awake long enough learns its sounds. There is the hum of the refrigeration units behind the supermarket, which never stops, and the click and whir of the traffic lights changing at the empty crossroads. There is the last bus at a quarter past midnight and the first delivery lorry at five. There are foxes, which scream in a way that frightens visitors, and owls in the churchyard trees, and in summer a blackbird that starts singing an hour before dawn.

The harbour adds its own layer. Halyards slap against the masts of the moored yachts whenever the wind gets up. The fishing boats go out on the tide, whatever the hour, and their engines start one after another with a low cough and settle into a steady beat as they pass the breakwater. On foggy nights the horn on the headland sounds every thirty seconds, a deep, sad note that carries through closed windows into every bedroom in the lower town.

People who move to the town from a city often say that they cannot sleep at first because it is too quiet. After a few weeks they say the opposite: that they cannot sleep in the city any more because it is too loud. What has changed is not the noise but what they notice. The mind learns which sounds matter and which can be ignored. A fog horn that kept a newcomer awake on the first night becomes, after a month, a sound that the sleeper hears without waking and would miss if it stopped.

The old keeper of the lower light wrote about this in the only part of her log that is not about weather or ships. On a page near the end of her last year, below an entry about a calm night with light airs from the west, she added one line: that she had slept through the fog horn for thirty years, but had woken every time the lamp above her went dark.
